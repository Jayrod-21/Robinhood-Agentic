## Bull
**AMD – Buy Case**

AMD is structurally positioned to capture a disproportionate share of the AI accelerator TAM as hyperscalers aggressively diversify away from single-vendor GPU dependency — MI300X/MI325X adoption at Microsoft, Meta, and Oracle is already generating real, accelerating data center revenue, not speculation. The company's unified CPU-GPU-APU architecture and open ROCm software stack are maturing fast enough to close the competitive gap with CUDA for inference workloads, which is where the next wave of hyperscaler capex is concentrating. AMD's x86 server CPU business (EPYC Genoa/Turin) continues taking socket share from Intel on the back of genuine IPC and power-efficiency advantages, providing a durable, high-margin cash flow base that funds the AI buildout without dilution. The forward catalyst is discrete and falsifiable: MI325X/MI350X ramp into 2025 hyperscaler procurement cycles should drive data center segment revenue well above $5B annually, with gross margin expansion as software-attached, higher-ASP accelerator revenue grows as a mix percentage. The thesis is proven wrong if ROCm fails to achieve workload portability across top-10 AI model frameworks at scale, if hyperscaler MI300-series reorder rates stall in back-to-back quarters, or if AMD loses meaningful EPYC socket share to ARM-based alternatives faster than data center AI revenue offsets it. At a reasonable entry — sub-20x forward EV/EBIT on trough estimates — you are paying for the CPU franchise and getting the AI accelerator optionality near free. The position sizing argument is straightforward: this is one of two companies in the world with the silicon and software stack to credibly challenge NVIDIA's AI compute monopoly.

## Bear
**The Case to Avoid/Sell AMD**

AMD's bull thesis rests almost entirely on data-center GPU momentum eating into NVIDIA's AI training and inference dominance, but the fundamental problem is that AMD has not demonstrated it can hold enterprise software ecosystems — ROCm remains materially inferior to CUDA, and hyperscalers building AMD GPU clusters are doing so as negotiating leverage against NVIDIA, not as a primary stack. When those contracts get stress-tested by actual developer workflows, AMD loses the stickiness argument. On the core CPU side, Intel's Panther Lake and future process improvements represent a real re-entry threat just as AMD's PC and server CPU share gains begin to plateau, compressing the one business line with proven, durable margins. AMD's valuation — historically trading at 30-50x forward earnings during momentum phases — prices in a GPU ramp that requires both ROCm software adoption and sustained hyperscaler capex that is itself vulnerable to any AI monetization disappointment. The falsifiable mechanism: if AMD's MI300X/MI350 series fails to show accelerating external revenue (non-hyperscaler, independent software vendor adoption) in the next two earnings cycles, the GPU narrative deflates without the CPU business being strong enough to hold the multiple. What would prove this wrong is clear: ROCm developer mindshare data, ISV certifications, and MI-series revenue crossing $10B+ annualized with diverse customer concentration. Until that evidence exists, you are paying a premium for a competitive dynamic that NVIDIA controls and AMD does not.
